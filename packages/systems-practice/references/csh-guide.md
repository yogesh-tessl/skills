## Core Idea

Critical Systems Heuristics, developed by Werner Ulrich, is based on a simple but powerful insight: **every claim about what "should" be done rests on boundary judgments** — decisions about what is included in the analysis and what is left out.

These boundary judgments are often invisible. When someone says "we need to improve efficiency," they have already made boundary judgments about: whose efficiency matters, who gets to define efficiency, what counts as evidence, and who bears the cost of the "improvement."

CSH makes these invisible boundaries visible by asking 12 systematic questions across four dimensions: Motivation, Power, Knowledge, and Legitimacy. Each question is asked in two modes:

- **IS mode**: What is the case? (descriptive — how things currently work)
- **OUGHT mode**: What ought to be the case? (normative — how things should work)

The gap between IS and OUGHT is where critical reflection happens.

## The 12 Boundary Questions

### Motivation (Sources of Purpose)

| # | IS | OUGHT |
|---|-----|-------|
| 1 | Who is the **actual client** — whose interests are currently served? | Who **ought to be** the client — whose interests should be served? |
| 2 | What is the **actual purpose** — what is currently being achieved? | What **ought to be** the purpose — what should the system achieve? |
| 3 | What is the built-in **measure of success** — how is improvement currently judged? | What **ought to be** the measure of success — how should we judge improvement? |

**What these reveal**: Purpose often gets captured by those with power. The stated purpose ("improve patient care") may differ from the actual purpose ("reduce costs"). Measures of success tend to reflect the values of whoever designed them.

### Power (Sources of Control)

| # | IS | OUGHT |
|---|-----|-------|
| 4 | Who is the **actual decision-maker** — who currently has the power to change the situation? | Who **ought to be** the decision-maker — who should have this power? |
| 5 | What **resources** are currently controlled and by whom? | What resources **ought to be** available and to whom? |
| 6 | What **conditions of success** are currently part of the system's environment (i.e., taken as given)? | What conditions **ought to be** under the control of the system rather than treated as external? |

**What these reveal**: Power asymmetries hide in "environmental constraints." When someone says "the budget is fixed," they are making a boundary judgment about what is negotiable and what isn't — and that judgment usually protects the interests of those who set the budget.

### Knowledge (Sources of Expertise)

| # | IS | OUGHT |
|---|-----|-------|
| 7 | Who is considered the **expert** — whose knowledge currently counts? | Who **ought to be** considered an expert — whose knowledge should count? |
| 8 | What counts as **relevant knowledge** — what currently passes as evidence? | What **ought to count** as relevant knowledge? |
| 9 | What is the **guarantee of success** — what currently assures that things will work? | What **ought to be** the guarantee — on what basis should we believe this will work? |

**What these reveal**: Expertise is socially constructed. In many situations, the people with the most direct experience (frontline workers, community members, patients) are not considered "experts" — while consultants, managers, or academics with less direct experience are. This question surfaces whose knowledge is being systematically undervalued.

### Legitimacy (Sources of Moral Authority)

| # | IS | OUGHT |
|---|-----|-------|
| 10 | Who is **affected** but has no voice in the current arrangement? | Who **ought to have** a voice but currently doesn't? |
| 11 | What worldview or value system **currently prevails** — what is treated as normal? | What worldview **ought to** prevail — whose values should guide decisions? |
| 12 | What gives the current arrangement its **legitimacy** — why do people accept it? | What **ought to** confer legitimacy — on what grounds should we accept the arrangement? |

**What these reveal**: Legitimacy is the deepest and most uncomfortable dimension. It asks why the current power structure is accepted. Often the answer is inertia, tradition, or the lack of visible alternatives — not genuine consent.

## IS vs OUGHT Analysis

The gap between IS and OUGHT is the analytical payoff. For each boundary question:

1. Answer the IS question based on evidence from the situation
2. Answer the OUGHT question based on the values and goals at stake
3. Describe the **gap** — how big is the discrepancy?
4. Assess the **consequence** — what does this gap mean for the situation?

Not all gaps are equal. Prioritize gaps that:
- Are large (big discrepancy between IS and OUGHT)
- Affect vulnerable populations (those with less power)
- Are invisible to current decision-makers (blind spots)
- Could lead to intervention failure if not addressed

## How to Use in Practice

### Start with the User's Language

Don't lead with "Let's do a boundary critique." Instead, ground the questions in what the user has already described:

- "You mentioned that [stakeholder X] isn't at the table. Let's explore what they're missing and who benefits from their absence."
- "The current plan assumes [Y]. What happens if that assumption is wrong? Whose interests does it protect?"

### Selective Depth

You don't always need all 12 questions. Focus on the dimensions where the user's situation has the most tension:

| If the situation involves... | Focus on... |
|------------------------------|-------------|
| Disagreement about goals | Motivation (Q1-Q3) |
| Centralized decision-making | Power (Q4-Q6) |
| Technical vs. local knowledge conflicts | Knowledge (Q7-Q9) |
| Marginalized or silent groups | Legitimacy (Q10-Q12) |

But always touch all four dimensions at least briefly — blind spots cluster in the dimensions you skip.

### Make It Concrete

Abstract boundary critique is useless. For every boundary judgment you surface, tie it to a specific consequence:

- Not just "frontline workers are excluded" → "frontline workers are excluded, which means the new workflow was designed without knowing that Step 3 takes 20 minutes, not 5, causing the entire schedule to break down on day one."

## Common Patterns

These patterns recur across many messy situations:

| Pattern | Description | What to Surface |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Expertise capture** | One type of knowledge dominates (usually quantitative/economic) | What qualitative, experiential, or local knowledge is being dismissed? |
| **Constraint laundering** | Political choices are presented as fixed constraints ("we can't change the budget") | Which "constraints" are actually decisions that could be revisited? |
| **Surrogate representation** | One group claims to speak for another ("we consulted the community") | Did the represented group actually participate, or were they spoken for? |
| **Metric colonization** | The measure of success becomes the purpose (hitting targets vs. achieving outcomes) | Is the metric still serving the purpose, or has the purpose been replaced by the metric? |
| **Temporal exclusion** | Future stakeholders (next generation, future users) have no voice | Whose future interests are being traded away for present convenience? |

## Worked Example

**Situation**: A city government is implementing an AI-based predictive policing system.

### Boundary Critique (Selected Questions)

| Dimension | IS | OUGHT | Gap |
|-----------|-----|-------|-----|
| **Motivation** — Who benefits? | City government (efficiency), police leadership (resource allocation), vendor (contract revenue) | Residents in high-crime areas (safety without over-surveillance), all residents (fair treatment) | Current beneficiaries are institutional; affected communities are not primary beneficiaries |
| **Power** — Who decides? | Police chief selects deployment zones; vendor controls algorithm; city council approves budget | Affected neighborhoods should have input on deployment; independent body should audit algorithm | Deployment decisions are made without input from communities that experience the consequences |
| **Knowledge** — Whose expertise counts? | Vendor's data science team; crime statistics (historical arrest data) | Community knowledge of why crime concentrates in certain areas; sociological expertise on policing bias | Historical arrest data encodes past policing patterns (which neighborhoods were patrolled more), not actual crime distribution |
| **Legitimacy** — Who is affected but voiceless? | Residents of predicted "hot spots" (increased police presence); people with prior records (higher scrutiny); undocumented residents (fear of contact with police) | These groups should have formal representation in deployment decisions and algorithm auditing | The people most affected by the system have the least say in how it works |

### Assumptions Log

| # | Assumption | Held by | Evidence | Risk if wrong |
|---|-----------|---------|----------|---------------|
| 1 | Historical crime data reflects actual crime patterns | Vendor, police | Weak — data reflects arrest patterns, not crime patterns | Algorithm perpetuates biased policing |
| 2 | Increased police presence reduces crime | Police leadership | Mixed — depends on type of policing | May increase tension without reducing crime |
| 3 | Algorithm is race-neutral because race isn't an input | Vendor | Weak — geographic and socioeconomic proxies correlate with race | Indirect discrimination despite nominal neutrality |
| 4 | Public safety concerns override privacy concerns | City government | Contested — no public deliberation has occurred | System may lack democratic legitimacy |

### Excluded Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Why excluded | Impact | How to include |
|-------------|-------------|--------|----------------|
| Residents of deployment zones | No formal mechanism for input | Bear costs (surveillance, over-policing) without consenting | Community advisory board with veto power over deployment zones |
| Public defenders / civil liberties orgs | Not part of procurement process | Cannot challenge algorithm's use as evidence | Mandatory adversarial audit before deployment |
| Future residents | Cannot participate | Algorithm trained on current data shapes future enforcement | Sunset clause requiring revalidation every 2 years |
